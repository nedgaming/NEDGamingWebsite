Starting a High School Esports Team in the US

October 25, 2026 6 min read

From Club to Sanctioned Sport

High school esports in the United States has moved from lunchtime clubs to state-sanctioned activity with real championships. If you are a coach, teacher or athletic director weighing it up, the practical questions are the same ones every other sport answers — who sanctions it, what does it cost, and who is responsible.

The Landscape Shifts Every Season

Which states sanction esports, which leagues operate in them, and which titles are offered all change from one school year to the next. A state that had no sanctioned season two years ago may well run a championship now.

Before planning around any of it, confirm the current season directly with your state association and the league itself. Details in guides like this one age quickly.

Six Steps to a Season That Lasts

1

Check What Your State Already Runs

Start here, because it may decide everything else. Many state high school athletic and activity associations now sanction esports, often running their season on a third-party platform. The NFHS has partnered with PlayVS to bring esports to state associations, and individual associations — Kansas’ KSHSAA among the more recent — have launched their own sanctioned seasons with state championships.

If your state association sanctions esports, joining that league is almost always easier than building something independent: eligibility rules, schedules and a championship path already exist.

2

Know Your League Options

Where there is no state-sanctioned league, national organisations fill the gap. PlayVS runs scholastic leagues across many states. The High School Esports League (HSEL) and the Network of Academic and Scholastic Esports Federations (NASEF) offer competition alongside curriculum and club resources, with NASEF leaning strongly toward the educational side.

Season structures and the titles offered change year to year, so check the current season page rather than an article before you commit a roster.

3

Choose Titles Your School Can Defend

Game selection is a policy question as much as a competitive one. Scholastic leagues lean heavily on titles with lower age ratings — Rocket League, Mario Kart, Super Smash Bros., often Valorant at the high end — precisely because administrators and parents have to sign off.

Leading with a mature-rated shooter is the fastest way to lose a proposal. Start with what the league already sanctions.

4

Be Honest About Hardware

You need less than the marketing suggests, but more than a cart of library laptops. Machines that hold a stable frame rate in your chosen title, wired network, and peripherals that survive teenagers are the real requirements.

Many programs start by running a console title on existing displays, prove the activity works, and then ask for PCs with a season of participation numbers behind the request. That sequence gets funded far more often than an opening ask for a lab.

5

Get Administration Buy-In on Paper

Settle the governance questions before the first match, not during a dispute: who is the named supervising adult, what are the academic eligibility requirements, what is the conduct policy, how is communication with students handled, and does the activity sit under athletics or clubs?

Write it down. Esports programs rarely fail on competition; they fail when a situation arises that no one agreed a rule for.

6

Run the Season Like a Sport

Fixed practice times, a published schedule, attendance expectations and a roster with substitutes. Treating it casually is the quickest route to a team that evaporates by November.

Keep standings and results from week one. Participation data and a season record are what you will take to the administration when you ask for next year’s budget — and what a graduating senior will want when a college coach asks what they have played.
